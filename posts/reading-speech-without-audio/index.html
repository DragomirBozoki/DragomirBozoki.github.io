Reading Speech Without Audio

Building an end-to-end visual speech recognition system with computer vision, 3D CNNs, recurrent networks, and CTC decoding.

View source on GitHub →

Traditional speech recognition starts with a microphone. This project started with a camera. It was my Bachelor thesis in Biomedical Engineering, and the goal was to reconstruct spoken sentences using only the visual information contained in a speaker's lip movements. No audio, at any point, in training or inference.

Lipreading looks simple when humans do it badly, guessing at a word or two from context. For a neural network the problem is more structured than that, and also less forgiving. The model receives a sequence of images and has to produce a sequence of characters. There is no label saying which frame corresponds to which letter, and different phonemes can produce nearly identical mouth shapes. Sounds such as /p/, /b/, and /m/ all close the lips the same way. The information that tells them apart is mostly in the audio, not the video.

So the project is really three problems stacked on top of each other: extracting the visual information that actually matters from a video frame, modelling how that information changes over time, and converting a sequence of visual features into text without ever being told which frame maps to which character. Each of those problems pulls in a different part of the architecture.

System overview

At a high level, a video clip moves through a fixed pipeline before it ever reaches the network's first weight: 

Video
  -> Frame extraction
  -> Face detection
  -> Facial landmarks
  -> Lip ROI extraction
  -> 64x64 grayscale sequence
  -> 3D CNN
  -> Bidirectional GRU
  -> Character probabilities
  -> CTC decoding
  -> Predicted sentence

The first half of that pipeline is classical computer vision. The second half is the neural network. Neither half works without the other: feed the raw frame into the 3D CNN and it spends its capacity on background and lighting instead of lip shape, and feed a clean mouth crop into a model with no temporal structure and it loses the motion that lipreading actually depends on.

Dataset

Training data comes from the GRID audiovisual sentence corpus: 34 speakers, 1,000 utterances each, all following a fixed sentence grammar: 

command + color + preposition + letter + digit + adverb

which produces sentences such as "place red at C two now." Every recording is accompanied by a word-level alignment file, so the video and the transcript line up exactly. That fixed grammar is what makes GRID usable for a project like this. It is also, honestly, one of the project's built-in limitations: the vocabulary is small and the grammar never varies, which is a long way from unconstrained natural speech. I come back to that in the limitations section.

The GitHub repository's data pipeline is built to pool clips from twenty of GRID's speakers into one training set. The quantitative results later in this post, however, come from an earlier and narrower experiment: a model trained on a single speaker (s1), using the first 500 of their recordings, 450 for training and 50 held out for testing. I kept that distinction explicit throughout the post rather than blurring the two together.

Computer vision preprocessing

Raw video never touches the network directly. Every frame goes through the same sequence of steps, implemented with OpenCV and dlib:

  1. detect the face in the frame;
  2. estimate 68 facial landmark points;
  3. locate the mouth corners and top/bottom lip edges from four of those points;
  4. crop the region between them;
  5. resize the crop to 64 × 64;
  6. convert it to grayscale;
  7. normalize it against that clip's own pixel statistics;
  8. append it to the frame sequence in order.

The mouth crop specifically comes from landmark points 48 and 54 for the left and right corners of the lips, and points 50/51 and 58/59 for the top and bottom edges. That is a deliberately small, cheap piece of geometry, not a learned detector, and it is enough to isolate the region that matters.

Isolating the mouth like this matters because the network's capacity is limited and every parameter spent modelling something irrelevant is a parameter not spent modelling lip movement. Without this step the network would also have to learn to ignore:

  • background
  • clothing
  • hair
  • most of the rest of the face
  • head and camera motion

Cropping to the mouth region removes all of that before the network ever sees a pixel. Grayscale conversion happens for a similar reason: color carries very little information about what someone is saying compared to lip geometry, opening, and motion, and dropping two of three channels cuts the input size for free.

Here is the core of that crop, simplified slightly from preprocessing.py:

faces = detector(gray_frame)
for face in faces:
    landmarks = predictor(gray_frame, face)

    lip_left = landmarks.part(48).x
    lip_right = landmarks.part(54).x
    lip_top = min(landmarks.part(50).y, landmarks.part(51).y)
    lip_bottom = max(landmarks.part(58).y, landmarks.part(59).y)

    lip_frame = frame[lip_top:lip_bottom, lip_left:lip_right]
    lip_frame_resized = cv2.resize(lip_frame, (64, 64))

One detail worth calling out: if the face detector fails on a given frame, that frame is simply skipped rather than interpolated or replaced. A short run of missed detections quietly shortens the sequence before it ever reaches padding. It is a small thing, but it is the kind of small thing that determines whether a pipeline is robust in practice, not just on paper.

After cropping, each frame's own mean and standard deviation are used to normalize it, rather than a fixed constant computed once over the whole dataset. That keeps the normalization adapted to each clip's own lighting and contrast instead of assuming every recording looks the same.

Temporal representation

One input sample is a fixed-length sequence:

75 x 64 x 64 x 1

where 75 is the number of frames, 64 × 64 is the spatial resolution of each cropped mouth region, and 1 is the single grayscale channel. The temporal dimension is not incidental. A single static mouth frame is often ambiguous on its own: a rounded, mostly-closed mouth could be mid-word in several different ways. What disambiguates it is the transition around it. 

closed lips
  -> opening
  -> rounded configuration

That three-step sequence carries far more information than any single frame in it. Keeping frames in order, rather than treating them as an unordered bag of images, is what makes that motion visible to the network at all.

Model architecture

The network combines two components with different jobs: a 3D convolutional stack that extracts spatiotemporal visual features, and a pair of bidirectional recurrent layers that model how those features evolve across the full sequence. 

75 x 64 x 64 x 1 video
  -> Conv3D (128 filters)
  -> MaxPool3D
  -> Conv3D (256 filters)
  -> MaxPool3D
  -> Conv3D (64 filters)
  -> MaxPool3D
  -> TimeDistributed Flatten
  -> Bidirectional GRU (128 units)
  -> Bidirectional GRU (128 units)
  -> Dense + Softmax (41 classes)
  -> 75 x 41 probability sequence
  -> CTC decoder
  -> text

All three Conv3D layers use 3 × 3 × 3 kernels and ReLU activations. Pooling is applied as 1 × 2 × 2, meaning it shrinks the spatial dimensions but deliberately leaves the temporal dimension untouched, since the recurrent layers downstream need that full 75-step sequence intact. After the convolutional stack, a TimeDistributed flatten turns each time step's spatial feature map into a single vector, independently, before the sequence is handed to the GRUs. Both GRU layers use 128 units with orthogonal weight initialization and 0.5 dropout, and the final dense layer outputs a softmax distribution over 41 character classes at every one of the 75 time steps.

Why 3D convolutions?

A standard 2D convolution learns patterns inside a single image: edges, gradients, local shapes. A 3D convolution learns patterns across time × height × width at once, which means it can respond directly to short movements, not just static configurations. Lips closing, a jaw dropping, a mouth rounding over two or three frames, these are the kind of short transitions a 2D convolution simply cannot see, because it never looks at more than one frame at a time.

That distinction matters here specifically because a single frame under-determines the sound being produced. Two different phonemes can leave the mouth in visually identical positions. What separates them is often the motion on either side of that position, and 3D convolutions are what let the network pick that motion up before anything reaches the recurrent layers.

Why recurrent layers?

Short-range motion from the convolutional stack is still not the whole picture. A particular mouth movement can be ambiguous in isolation and only resolve once you see more of the sentence around it. The two Bidirectional GRU layers exist for that: each time step's representation is built from both directions. 

past -> current frame <- future

This works specifically because the model operates on complete, already-recorded clips rather than a live stream. A real-time system would only have access to the past at inference time and would need a different design. Here, the whole 75-frame sequence is available before decoding starts, so there is no reason not to let later frames inform earlier ones.

The alignment problem

Every clip has 75 video frames. Most GRID sentences have only a few dozen characters. There is no ground truth saying frames 1 through 4 are the letter "p," frames 5 through 8 are "l," and so on. Producing that kind of frame-level label by hand, across a dataset this size, would be impractical and more or less pointless. The alignment between input length and output length has to be learned, not annotated.

CTC decoding

This is what Connectionist Temporal Classification solves. The network predicts a character distribution at every one of the 75 time steps, plus one extra option: a blank symbol that means "nothing new here." A raw, conceptual model output might look like: 

_ _ p p _ l l _ a a _ c c e _

Collapse the repeated characters and drop the blanks, and what is left is:

place

That collapsing rule is what lets the model be trained directly on video → text pairs without anyone ever specifying which frame corresponds to which letter. Training uses TensorFlow's ctc_batch_cost, which compares the predicted probability sequence against the target transcript while summing over every alignment that could have produced it, not just one fixed guess.

Training

The network is trained with the Adam optimizer at an initial learning rate of 1e-4. A custom scheduler holds that rate constant for the first 50 epochs, then, every 50-epoch cycle after that, alternates between holding steady and applying an exponential decay. The full schedule in the repository targets 500 epochs, with weights checkpointed after every epoch based on training loss.

Two callbacks made the training loop easier to trust. One decodes and prints a batch of predictions against their ground truth at the end of every epoch, so I could actually read what the model thought it heard, not just watch a loss number go down. A falling CTC loss tells you the model is getting more confident; it does not tell you whether it is confident about the right thing. Seeing "place white at three soon" where the target was "place white at d three soon" is a five-second sanity check that a loss curve alone cannot give you. The other callback writes the running training history to disk every few epochs, so a long run surviving an interruption does not cost the whole training log.

The repository's data pipeline pools clips from twenty named GRID speakers into a single shuffled training set, batched at 512 clips split across 8 sub-batches for multi-GPU training through TensorFlow's MirroredStrategy. That is a different, larger configuration than the single-speaker run the numbers below come from, and I do not have a directly comparable measurement for it, only the design intent: reduce how specialized the model becomes to any one person's face and articulation.

Speaker generalization

This is one of the harder parts of lipreading in general, not just in this project. Different people do not just sound different, they move differently. Lip geometry, jaw shape, articulation habits, speaking speed, and how much someone moves their head all vary from speaker to speaker, and none of that variation is something the grammar of GRID controls for.

The goal of training on multiple speakers is to push the network toward representations tied to speech itself rather than to any one person's mouth. Whether that goal was actually reached is a measurement question, and I only have a real measurement for the narrower, single-speaker configuration, which is what the next section covers honestly rather than optimistically.

What made this harder than expected

Viseme ambiguity

Sounds like /p/, /b/, and /m/ can look almost identical on the lips even though they sound completely different. Visually, a viseme can map to several different phonemes, and no amount of clean preprocessing removes that ambiguity, since it is not a preprocessing problem, it is a property of the task.

Missing information

Some sounds are shaped mostly by the tongue or the vocal cords and leave almost no visible trace on the lips at all. Visual speech recognition is fundamentally working with partial information, not a noisy version of the full signal.

Preprocessing dependency

The whole system is only as reliable as its face and landmark detection. If those fail on a frame, or drift slightly, everything downstream, the crop, the normalization, the sequence the network trains on, inherits that error. There is no recovery mechanism later in the pipeline.

Temporal alignment

Covered above, but worth repeating as a standalone difficulty: input and output sequences have different, unrelated lengths, and that mismatch has to be handled by the loss function, not worked around in preprocessing.

Speaker variability

The same sentence, spoken by two different people, can produce visibly different motion. A model that performs well on one speaker and poorly on another has not necessarily learned less, it may simply have learned something narrower than intended.

Results

The numbers below come from the single-speaker experiment described in the dataset section: trained on speaker s1's first 450 clips for 60 epochs, then evaluated on 50 held-out clips from four different speakers.

Speaker WER (%) CER (%)
S1 (trained speaker) 12.10 7.21
S7 (unseen) 71.04 54.28
S9 (unseen) 68.22 51.09
S11 (unseen) 74.68 58.01

On the speaker it was trained on, the model held a word error rate around 12% and a character error rate around 7%, respectable for a from-scratch architecture on a constrained grammar. On speakers it had never seen, word error rate jumped past 68% across the board. That gap is the single clearest result in the whole project, and it is not subtle.

The most common failure mode was dropping alveolar consonants, sounds like /d/, /n/, /t/, /s/, and /l/, that are visually close to each other because they all involve contact between the tongue and the ridge behind the upper teeth, which the camera cannot see. Two representative examples from the unseen-speaker test set:

target: "lay blue at d six now" → predicted: "lay blue at six now"
target: "place white at d three soon" → predicted: "place white at three soon"

In both cases the model dropped the letter entirely rather than substituting a wrong one, which fits with alveolar consonants being close to invisible from the lips. I'd attribute the generalization gap to two compounding causes rather than one: 60 epochs is a short training run for this architecture, and 450 clips from a single speaker is a narrow basis for learning anything speaker-independent. Both are addressable with more compute and more data. Neither is addressable by tuning the model further on the same 450 clips.

Limitations

  • GRID uses a fixed, constrained grammar. Unrestricted natural speech is a considerably harder problem than anything measured here.
  • GRID recordings are controlled: consistent lighting, a fixed camera position, a plain background. Real-world video is messier on every one of those axes.
  • The system depends entirely on reliable face and mouth detection. Large head rotation or occlusion degrades everything downstream of it.
  • Visual information alone cannot uniquely determine every phoneme. Some of what distinguishes spoken words is simply not visible on the lips.
  • The architecture predates modern large-scale visual speech systems and is far smaller than what current published work uses.
  • This is a research prototype from a Bachelor thesis, not a production speech recognition system, and I would not describe it as one.

What I would do differently today

The recurrent layers here are a reasonable choice for a first implementation, but I would want to compare them against a Transformer or Conformer sequence encoder today. Self-attention can model longer-range dependencies more directly than a GRU has to, one recurrent step at a time, and Conformers in particular were built specifically for combining local and global structure in speech.

I would also look at stronger visual encoders than three plain Conv3D blocks, and at pairing CTC decoding with an external language model, since a language model can rule out visually plausible but linguistically nonsensical decodings the acoustic side of the system has no way to reject on its own.

Beyond the architecture, the more interesting extension is audio-visual fusion: instead of video → text, a model that takes both audio + video → text and leans on whichever modality is more reliable at a given moment. That is closer to how the visual channel is actually useful in practice, as a backup signal for noisy audio rather than a full replacement for it. And a real deployment would need real-time inference: face tracking instead of per-frame detection, a rolling buffer instead of a fixed 75-frame clip, streaming decoding, and enough latency optimization to run on something like an embedded Jetson rather than a training workstation.

What I learned

The clearest thing this project taught me is that visual speech recognition is not image classification wearing a trench coat. Useful information here exists at several different levels at once: raw pixels, facial geometry, short-term motion, longer sequence context, and finally the alignment between that sequence and actual characters. None of those levels is optional, and the hard part of the project was never any one of them individually, it was connecting all of them into one pipeline that trains end to end.

Doing that connected computer vision, deep learning, sequence modelling, and a bit of NLP into a single working system, and doing it myself, end to end, taught me more about where systems like this actually break than reading about any one of those pieces in isolation ever did.
