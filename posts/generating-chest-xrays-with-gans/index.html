Generating Chest X-rays with Generative Adversarial Networks

Training a GAN to learn the visual structure of medical radiographs.

Generative models are often demonstrated on faces, landscapes, or small image datasets. I wanted to work on a problem where mistakes are easier to notice and where image structure actually matters.

Chest X-rays were a good fit.

Unlike many natural images, radiographs have a relatively consistent global composition: the thoracic cavity occupies a predictable region, the lungs are approximately symmetric, the spine creates a strong central structure, and ribs, clavicles, and surrounding tissue form repeating anatomical patterns.

At the same time, generating a convincing X-ray is far from trivial. A model can quickly learn that an X-ray is a mostly dark grayscale image with a bright region in the middle. Learning the actual anatomical relationships is considerably harder.

This project became an experiment in whether a convolutional GAN could progressively learn those structures directly from a dataset of real chest radiographs.

The implementation was built in Python using TensorFlow and Keras, with a custom generator, discriminator, and training loop rather than relying on a pre-built GAN framework.

The objective

The basic task was straightforward: given a random latent vector, generate a new chest X-ray that follows the visual distribution of real radiographs.

The model does not copy an existing patient image and it is not given a specific X-ray as input. Instead, the generator starts from a 256-dimensional random vector sampled from a normal distribution. That vector is gradually transformed into a grayscale image.

The discriminator receives either a real radiograph or a generated one and attempts to determine which one it is seeing. Training therefore becomes a competition. The discriminator continuously improves at detecting synthetic images, while the generator continuously adapts to produce images that are more difficult to distinguish from the real dataset. 

random noise -> generator -> synthetic X-ray -> discriminator -> real / fake

This adversarial feedback is the only supervision the generator receives.

Dataset and preprocessing

I used chest radiographs derived from the NIH Chest X-ray dataset as the basis of the experiment. Before training, the images had to be converted into a consistent representation suitable for the neural network.

The preprocessing pipeline included resizing, grayscale handling, and normalization. I experimented with different resolutions during development, while the final model architecture operates on 512 × 512 grayscale images.

Using a relatively high output resolution made the task substantially harder than the typical 64 × 64 or 128 × 128 GAN examples. A 512 × 512 grayscale image already contains more than 260,000 output pixels. More importantly, those pixels cannot be generated independently. The model has to preserve meaningful spatial relationships across the complete image.

I also implemented conservative augmentation. For medical images I deliberately avoided aggressive spatial transformations. Random rotations, crops, or geometric distortions can introduce anatomically questionable images and can change relationships that the network should actually learn.

Instead, augmentation is limited primarily to small changes in:

  • brightness
  • contrast

The changes are intentionally small, around ±8%, with the goal of increasing appearance variability without modifying anatomical geometry.

Augmentation should improve robustness without generating unrealistic medical samples. That principle shaped every preprocessing decision in this project.

Designing the generator

The generator is responsible for the most difficult part of the system. It receives a latent vector, z ∈ ℝ256, and has to convert it into an entire radiograph.

I start by projecting the 256-dimensional latent representation using a dense layer into a 4 × 4 × 1024 feature tensor. From there, the image is progressively expanded: 

4x4 -> 8x8 -> 16x16 -> 32x32 -> 64x64 -> 128x128 -> 256x256 -> 512x512

Each stage increases the spatial resolution while convolutional layers learn increasingly detailed representations.

The early layers are responsible for very coarse structure. At this point the network is effectively deciding where the large bright and dark regions of the image should exist. Later layers begin modelling finer details such as:

  • the outline of the lung fields
  • the central mediastinal region
  • shoulder and clavicle structure
  • rib-like textures
  • intensity transitions between tissue regions

I used relatively wide convolution kernels in the early generator layers and gradually reduced the kernel size toward the output. This gives the early stages a larger receptive field for learning global structure while allowing later layers to focus more on local image detail.

Batch normalization is used throughout the generator to stabilize the intermediate feature distributions. LeakyReLU activations are used instead of standard ReLU in the majority of the architecture, while the final layer uses a tanh activation. This maps the generated output to the same normalized range used for the training images.

Another part of the architecture that I experimented with was light dropout inside the generator. Rather than treating the generator as a deterministic image decoder during training, a small amount of stochastic regularization helps prevent individual feature channels from becoming overly dominant.

The discriminator

The discriminator performs the opposite transformation. Instead of expanding a compact representation into an image, it progressively compresses a 512 × 512 radiograph into a single probability. The convolutional stack uses increasing channel counts: 

32 -> 64 -> 128 -> 256 -> 512

while stride-2 convolutions gradually reduce the spatial resolution. After the convolutional feature extractor, the representation is flattened and passed through an additional dense layer before producing the final sigmoid output. The result is one number, D(x) ∈ [0,1], where a value close to 1 indicates that the discriminator believes the image came from the real dataset.

Dropout is used relatively aggressively in the discriminator. This was useful because one common failure mode during GAN training is that the discriminator learns faster than the generator and becomes nearly perfect. When that happens, the generator receives very little useful gradient information. A slightly regularized discriminator therefore turned out to be preferable to an unrestricted classifier that immediately dominates the adversarial game.

Writing the training loop

One of the more useful parts of this project was implementing the GAN training logic myself. Instead of simply calling model.fit() on a conventional supervised model, I implemented a custom train_step using TensorFlow's GradientTape.

Every training iteration contains two separate optimization problems.

First, the discriminator is updated. A batch of real chest X-rays is taken from the dataset and a batch of random latent vectors is sampled. The generator converts those vectors into synthetic X-rays. The discriminator then evaluates both sets, D(real images) and D(generated images). Binary cross-entropy is used for the discriminator loss.

After the discriminator update, a new latent batch is sampled and the generator is trained. This time the generator's objective is effectively to produce images for which the discriminator predicts "real." The discriminator weights are not updated during this part of the step. Its predictions simply provide the learning signal that propagates back through the generator.

Separating these two optimization steps made it much easier to experiment with the balance between the networks.

Stabilizing GAN training

The architecture itself was only part of the problem. Most of the work in GAN experiments happens after the model technically runs. A GAN can train without crashing while still producing useless images.

Some of the problems I encountered are typical for adversarial training:

  • the discriminator becoming too strong
  • the generator collapsing toward similar samples
  • unstable oscillation between generator and discriminator losses
  • images improving for a period and later degrading
  • globally X-ray-like outputs with anatomically poor detail

I introduced several mechanisms to make the training process less brittle.

Label smoothing

The discriminator is not trained with perfectly hard labels. Instead of forcing every real image toward exactly 1 and every synthetic image toward exactly 0, I slightly smooth the labels. This reduces discriminator overconfidence and produces a more useful learning signal for the generator.

Random label flipping

A small percentage of labels are deliberately flipped during discriminator training. This introduces noise into what would otherwise become an extremely deterministic classification problem. The probability is intentionally low, around 5%, so the discriminator still learns the correct task without becoming excessively confident.

Dropout

Dropout is applied to multiple discriminator layers and lightly inside the generator. In practice this provided additional regularization and helped prevent one network from becoming overly specialized against the current state of the other.

Separate generator and discriminator updates

The training code explicitly computes gradients for each model independently. That allowed me to modify learning rates and optimizer behaviour separately when continuing the training later.

Monitoring the model through generated images

GAN losses are useful, but they are not sufficient. A lower generator loss does not necessarily mean that the generated X-rays look better. For this type of model, visual progression is one of the most informative debugging tools.

I therefore implemented a training callback that periodically samples new latent vectors and saves generated radiographs. Early during training I saved images frequently so that changes were easy to inspect. Later, once changes between individual epochs became smaller, the sampling interval was increased. Model checkpoints were also saved periodically so that useful training states were not lost if later adversarial training became unstable.

This turned training into something closer to an experiment log. Instead of only asking what the losses were at epoch 2000, I could inspect what the model actually believed an X-ray looked like at epochs 500, 1000, 2000, and 3000. The visual evolution was much more informative.

What the model learns first

One of the most interesting parts of the project was observing the order in which image structure appeared. At the beginning, generated samples were essentially structured noise. The model first started discovering the broad intensity distribution of radiographs: images became dark around the edges with brighter structures concentrated around the thorax.

After that, recognizable global anatomy began to emerge. The network learned that chest X-rays tend to contain two large lung regions separated by a central structure. Only later did finer patterns begin appearing inside those regions.

This illustrates an important property of convolutional generative networks. The generator does not learn "lungs," "ribs," or "spine" as explicitly labelled objects. None of those structures are annotated for the GAN. They emerge because reproducing them helps the generator match the distribution that the discriminator sees in real radiographs.

Continuing training from later checkpoints

The initial training pipeline was deliberately simple. Both networks used Adam optimizers with the same learning rate and were trained through the custom adversarial loop.

Once the generator had already learned the basic X-ray distribution, I created a separate fine-tuning stage rather than simply continuing with the exact same configuration. The fine-tuning pipeline loads the generator and discriminator checkpoints from around epoch 3000 and resumes training with a different optimization strategy.

At that point, the problem is no longer simply learning what an X-ray looks like. The network already knows that. The objective becomes improving details without destroying the global structure that has already been learned.

For that reason, I reduced the generator learning rate relative to the discriminator. The fine-tuning configuration uses approximately: 

Generator LR     = 5e-5
Discriminator LR = 1e-4

The idea was to make generator updates more conservative during this stage.

Exponential Moving Average

Another improvement introduced during fine-tuning was an Exponential Moving Average (EMA) version of the generator. Instead of using only the generator weights from the current optimization step, EMA maintains a second set of weights: 

theta_EMA <- alpha * theta_EMA + (1 - alpha) * theta
alpha ~= 0.999

The EMA generator therefore represents a slowly changing average of many previous generator states. GAN optimization can oscillate significantly, so the parameters from one particular epoch are not necessarily the best parameters for inference. Averaging them over time often produces visually more stable outputs.

During fine-tuning, generated monitoring images are therefore sampled using the EMA generator when available. I also save the EMA model independently from the normal generator and discriminator checkpoints. This made it possible to compare the current generator, the moving-average generator, and earlier checkpoints, rather than assuming that the final epoch must automatically contain the best model.

Mixed precision and GPU training

The later training pipeline also enables TensorFlow mixed precision. Generating 512 × 512 images through several convolutional stages is significantly more expensive than working with the smaller image sizes commonly used in introductory GAN examples. Mixed precision allows supported GPU operations to use lower-precision floating-point formats where appropriate, reducing memory pressure and improving throughput.

I also configured TensorFlow GPU memory growth so that the runtime does not immediately reserve all available GPU memory. These changes did not alter the conceptual model, but they became increasingly important as the image resolution and training duration increased.

Results

The most obvious improvement can be seen by comparing early and later generated samples. Around the earlier training stages, the model captures the basic grayscale appearance of radiographs but the anatomy is weak and inconsistent. After extended training, the images show significantly more organized thoracic structure. The lung regions become clearer, the central anatomy becomes more stable, and the overall intensity distribution is much closer to the source dataset.

The model is still an experimental generative system rather than a clinically validated medical imaging model. That distinction is important. A synthetic image can look convincing while still containing anatomically incorrect or medically impossible details. For that reason, the generated images should be interpreted as a study of generative modelling and synthetic data generation, not as diagnostic radiographs.

Evaluating a medical GAN

Evaluating generative models is significantly less straightforward than evaluating a classifier. There is no simple equivalent of classification accuracy. I used visual comparison as the primary tool during development and also explored quantitative comparisons between real and generated image distributions, including pixel-intensity histograms and image-distance measurements.

Such measurements are useful for identifying obvious distribution differences, but they are not sufficient to determine whether generated anatomy is realistic. A stronger evaluation pipeline would include metrics such as FID and SSIM, ideally combined with feature representations that are meaningful specifically for radiology rather than generic natural images.

For a serious medical synthetic-data system, I would also want downstream validation. For example:

  1. train a diagnostic classifier using only real images;
  2. train the same classifier using real images plus generated samples;
  3. evaluate both on a completely untouched real test set.

If synthetic samples genuinely improve generalization, that provides stronger evidence that the generator has learned useful structure rather than simply producing images that look plausible.

What I would change today

If I continued the project, I would probably move away from a completely unconditional GAN. The existing generator learns a single distribution, P(X-ray), but many interesting medical applications require something closer to P(X-ray | condition).

A conditional GAN could use disease labels to control generation. For example, the model could be conditioned on:

  • normal
  • pneumonia
  • cardiomegaly
  • pleural effusion
  • other radiological findings

This would make the generated data significantly more useful for controlled augmentation experiments. I would also explore architectures designed specifically for more stable high-resolution generation, alongside diffusion-based models.

The project was built around GANs intentionally, because understanding the adversarial training process was one of the goals. Today, however, I would compare the results against a diffusion baseline rather than assuming a GAN is automatically the best generative architecture for the problem.

What I learned from the project

The most important lesson was that building a GAN architecture is relatively easy. Making two networks learn at compatible speeds is the difficult part. A generator and discriminator can both be technically correct while the complete system fails. Small changes in regularization, learning rates, checkpoint selection, and training strategy can have a disproportionate effect on the results.

I also learned how different medical image generation is from ordinary computer vision augmentation. With natural photographs, fairly aggressive geometric transformations are often acceptable. With medical data, altering geometry can change the meaning of the image. That forced me to think more carefully about preprocessing and augmentation instead of applying standard image pipelines automatically.

Finally, training at 512 × 512 made the hierarchical nature of image generation very visible. The network learns broad structure long before fine structure. Watching that progression across thousands of training epochs was probably the most useful part of the entire experiment.
